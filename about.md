# About PhenoChange

## Why vegetative phenology matters

Vegetative phenology—the seasonal timing of leaf production and loss—is a fundamental control on ecosystem function. It influences the exchange of carbon, water and energy between vegetation and the atmosphere, making it central to understanding how tropical forests and savannas influence and respond to environmental change. Despite its importance, vegetative phenology remains poorly understood across much of the tropics, particularly in Africa and South America, where long-term ground-based observations remain scarce.

## What is PhenoChange?

**PhenoChange is a network for monitoring the phenology of forests and savannas.**

The network uses repeated ground-based digital photography to monitor seasonal vegetation change at long-term research sites across Africa and South America. By applying standardized monitoring methods across diverse climates and ecosystems, PhenoChange enables direct comparisons of phenological behaviour across continents and provides the foundation for long-term international monitoring.

## Why use ground-based cameras?

Satellite observations have transformed our ability to monitor vegetation globally, but they cannot always distinguish between tree and ground-layer vegetation and may miss important seasonal transitions because of cloud cover or limited spatial and temporal resolution. Ground-based cameras complement satellite observations and human observation by providing continuous, high-resolution records of vegetation change throughout the year.

## From individual plants to ecosystems

PhenoChange monitors the seasonal dynamics of individual tree crowns, ground-layer vegetation and entire ecosystems. These observations are combined with satellite products and environmental data to improve our understanding of how tropical forests and savannas respond to climate variability and environmental change.

By linking standardized ground observations across continents, PhenoChange provides a unique resource for understanding the drivers of vegetative phenology and improving predictions of how tropical ecosystems will respond to future global change.
